How to play

Mini golf, three dimensions, four clubs. Ninety seconds of reading.

  1. Pull, and let go. Press anywhere on the course and pull away from where you want the ball to go, like a catapult. How far you pull is how hard you hit — the ring round the ball fills up as it winds on, and turns red when there is nothing left to give. The direction is simply the reverse of your pull, so one drag aims anywhere, all the way round, and the camera holds still while you do it. Let go to play the shot. The finger that starts the pull owns it: a second one landing on the glass will not move your aim.
  2. Pick a club. Click the bag in the bottom corner: the clubs come out, line up and turn slowly so you can look at them, and the one under your pointer is explained above. Click a head to take it, or swipe across the bag to step to the next one without opening it. The heads are set at their real lofts, so the wedge lying right back is the one that goes up. Keys 1–4 take a club directly, C steps to the next one, and B opens the bag. The putter rolls flat and stops where you tell it, the driver is the reach club, the chipper hops a rail and keeps running, and the wedge goes up and over water and sand. Each one has its own ceiling on power, so a full swing means something different in every hand.
  3. Read the hole first. V lifts the camera above the whole hole; scroll or pinch to zoom. The dotted lines in front of the ball are where the shot is headed — real physics, not a guess, so they tell you the truth about rails and ramps. There are three of them rather than one: a lane of a bit less power, a bit more, and what is loaded right now, because touch is too coarse to load an exact number and the game should not pretend otherwise. Aim the lane at a rail and it carries on through the bounce, fainter, on the way it comes off.
  4. Read the flag. The pin stands in the cup, and it is the only thing on the course telling you what the wind is doing: the flag streams away downwind, snaps in a gust, and hangs limp when the air goes still. The wind does not push the ball — a hole plays the same in a squall as it does at noon — but every hole has a sky of its own, and it is the same sky every time you come back to it. W changes it if you would rather see the hole in something else.
  5. Mind the water. A splash or a fall off the edge costs one stroke and you play again from where you hit. Sand is heavy going, boards are quick, and a tilted green breaks the ball downhill the whole way.
